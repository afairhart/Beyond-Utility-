01

Company Overview

Next TFX, Inc. is a Delaware C-corp, incorporated in early January 2026 and registered as a foreign corporation in California. Its principal office is at UCLA. It holds an exclusive worldwide license from The Regents of the University of California to membrane patent rights from Eric Hoek's lab, signed January 2026. At the time of investment it is pre-revenue and pre-product, with the membrane at lab and coupon scale.

The asset · membrane

BU1 · TFX membrane and modules

Sell and license the TFX membrane and spiral-wound modules into desalination and brackish-water plants. This is the licensed core, the only unit covered by the UCLA patent rights, and the business BUV is underwriting. About 9% of the company's projected 2035 revenue.

The ambition · minerals

BU2 · CMR brine refinery

A modular refinery that turns desalination reject brine into magnesium chloride plus recovered water, with the membrane as one stage. About 83% of projected 2035 revenue, mostly outside the UCLA license, capital-intensive, and not de-risked. Two further units, ion-exchange licensing and resale, round out the plan.

The central tension in one line

The defensible asset is about 9% of the projected business. The part driving the projections carries no moat from the asset BUV backed. The memo returns to this in Sections 05 and 06.

02

Market and Why Now

Reverse osmosis is the dominant technology in desalination and industrial water. The membrane is a small line item, but it sets the energy cost and recovery limit of the whole plant, which is what operators pay for. The specific pain Next TFX targets is real and quantified: under sustained high pressure, membranes compact, lose flux, and force the plant to push more energy until it is de-rated. A major manufacturer now lists compaction as a default in its own design software, which is the industry conceding the problem. A membrane that does not compact sells against a cost buyers already understand.

Why now, on the membrane side, is straightforward. Desalination capacity keeps expanding and brine and zero-liquid-discharge rules keep tightening, both pushing plants toward higher recovery and higher pressure. That is exactly the window where a compaction-resistant membrane matters most and incumbents are weakest.

Where I discount the market story

The biggest market on the page, critical minerals, is the one Next TFX is least equipped to serve today. The membrane enables brine concentration, but recovering lithium or magnesium is a chemistry and processing business the team has not run. Reading the membrane's market as the full lithium TAM overstates what the company can capture near term.

03

Technology and Product

The product is a thin-film crosslinked composite membrane, branded TFX. Conventional elements use a thin-film composite that the company describes as a stacked layer that compresses under pressure. TFX instead forms a crosslinked network, which the company argues gives it structural and chemical stability the standard structure cannot match. The same ultra-high-pressure membrane is the stage that makes the brine refinery conceivable, so the membrane is not a side asset to the mineral business. It is what makes the mineral business possible.

  • Less than 5% compaction loss at 120 bar, against 40 to 60% for competitor elements, and stable up to 200 bar.
  • More than 99% salt rejection at 180,000 mg/L, well into hypersaline territory.
  • Tolerance up to 85 degrees C, against roughly 45 degrees C for standard elements.
  • Manufacturable on existing equipment at or below competitor cost, with a stated drop-in fit.

The product is at TRL 4. The company offers two membrane families, one for seawater and one for brackish and concentrated brine, each with high, medium, and low recovery variants. The bench evidence behind these claims, and where it does and does not support the story, is in Section 08.

04

Team, the Core of the Bet

This is the strongest part of the company, and it is where the public record and the decks do not fully agree, so I lay it out carefully. On the membrane, founder-market fit is excellent and rare. On the brine refinery, the team has no earned operating credibility yet.

Eric M.V. Hoek, PhD, Co-founder, CEO, inventor. A UCLA Samueli professor and LBNL faculty scientist, one of the most cited membrane scientists in the world (30-plus patents, 170-plus papers, 25-plus years). A repeat founder whose NanoH2O commercialized a UCLA membrane and sold to LG. The deck claims seven ventures and roughly $1.3B in aggregate exits, which I have flagged to verify, but the NanoH2O outcome is real and is the best comparable for what Next TFX is trying to do. On the state filing he is the sole officer and sole director. By every legal measure, this is his company.

Patrick O'Brien, Co-founder, VP Operations. A UCLA Anderson MBA candidate (2026) and 12-year Army and DoD veteran. He runs the operational and commercial work and signs the UCLA license as co-founder, listed as the sole contact for notices, patents, and finances. Strong operating discipline, no prior water-industry operating background.

Team and governance items to close

Three things. First, the legal-versus-functional split: Hoek holds every officer title while O'Brien runs the day-to-day, so I want a clear org and a confirmed second executive. Second, Hoek is a full-time professor, so I need to understand how much of his time the company actually has. Third, a third name, Hikaru Watanabe, appears on the trademark filing, so the real team shape and equity split should be confirmed.

Key-person concentration

On paper Hoek is the company: sole officer, sole director, the inventor, and the source of the unlicensed manufacturing know-how. That concentration is the team risk I am taking, and it is why a confirmed second executive matters.

05

Intellectual Property and the UCLA License

The license is the asset, so I read it closely. It is a genuine exclusive license with real teeth, and it is thinner and more conditional than the phrase "exclusive UCLA license" suggests. Both things are true. The patent rights are two filings: a U.S. provisional from July 2023 that has since lapsed, and a PCT application from July 2024. There are no issued patents and no granted claims. The moat today is one pending application.

Two IP items to close before I treat the moat as solid

The PCT's 30-month national-phase deadline runs from the July 2023 priority date, which lands right around the investment window, so I need written confirmation that national-phase entries were filed and where. Separately, the license is patent-only. It excludes know-how, data, and tangible materials. The real protection is one pending patent plus unlicensed trade-secret knowledge that lives with the inventor.

06

Business Model and the Brine-Refinery Pivot

The membrane business is simple to underwrite: sell or license a better element into desalination and brackish plants, earn product margin, and let the UCLA royalty structure handle the licensing case. That is the business BUV backed. The brine refinery is the bigger and harder story. It takes reject brine, splits it, precipitates magnesium, washes out boron, dissolves it back into magnesium chloride, and recovers water through the ultra-high-pressure membrane, with a later phase generating its own reagents on site. The company models roughly $4.4M of revenue per plant.

I do not underwrite these numbers, and the memo should not repeat them to the IC as if they were ours. Three problems.

  • The margins are not real for the business described. A 93% EBITDA margin on a physical operation that precipitates, washes, filters, and dissolves commodity magnesium chloride near $459 a tonne is not how mineral processing works. That figure says the model is aspirational, not that the opportunity is large.
  • The mineral revenue is framed two incompatible ways. One deck sells magnesium chloride product per plant; another computes a 25% revenue share off a partner. Selling product and taking a revenue share are different businesses, and both appear in the same data room.
  • The demand is weakest where the revenue is largest. Commodity magnesium chloride for de-icing and dust control has substitutes and no forced demand. The lithium angle is more interesting but is not demonstrated anywhere in the data room.
How I want the IC to hold this

The brine refinery is genuinely clever systems thinking and the right kind of ambition for this membrane. It is not upside to bury, and it is not a plan to underwrite. It is the company's stated direction, it is mostly outside the licensed IP, it is capital-intensive, and it is not de-risked. The membrane is the bet. The refinery is the option on top of it.

Competition

On the membrane, the competitive set is the four incumbents who own commercial RO: LG/Hydranautics, Nitto, Toray, and DuPont. The point of the table is the operating window, not the absolute numbers. The opening on the membrane

Incumbents are not chasing this window

Their business is high-volume seawater and brackish RO at conventional pressures, and they have accepted compaction as a design assumption. That is what makes the beachhead narrow and hard to chase quickly, and why the likeliest outcome is one of them buying the technology rather than beating it. That is the NanoH2O pattern.

The harder field on minerals

Crowded and capital-heavy

Direct lithium extraction, magnesium recovery, and brine mining are crowded fields full of better-funded specialists. Next TFX would enter as a newcomer with no processing track record. This is another reason I weight the membrane over the refinery.

08

Traction and Validation

There is real bench evidence and real motion, and a long way still to go. The data room includes a head-to-head bench test of TFX against a Hydranautics commercial element, across seawater (35,000 mg/L, 60 bar), hypersaline (100,000 mg/L, 120 bar), and chemical-resistance soaks from pH 1 to 13. TFX holds above 99% rejection and stays stable after aggressive acid and base exposure, which supports the compaction and chemical-durability story.

What the data does and does not show

One honest nuance the deck does not lead with: in these runs, TFX's absolute water flow is lower than the Hydranautics element. The advantage is stability over time and after chemical attack, not higher flux. And this is coupon-scale, three-to-four-hour, in-house lab data. It is not spiral-wound module data, not long-duration, and not third-party verified. It is encouraging evidence at an early rung of the ladder, and it should be read that way.

The company has a March 2026 quote for a seawater RO pilot skid for a UCLA Integrated Pilot Study, with Next TFX supplying its own membranes, so the pilot is being built rather than just planned. The quoted skid tops out near 124 bar, below the 200-bar claim, so the first pilot will not test the full pressure envelope.

  • No signed customer LOI or customer-hosted pilot is in the data room yet. That is the 18-month UCLA milestone and the clearest near-term proof point.
  • No module-scale or third-party validation data yet. The jump from coupon to spiral-wound module at spec is where most membrane startups fail, so this is the data I most want next.

10

Pre-Mortem

It is two years out and this is a write-down. The likeliest stories, in order:
  • The membrane did not survive the jump to module scale. Coupon results did not reproduce in a spiral-wound element at spec. This is the classic membrane-startup death and the single most likely cause.
  • The company spread across four business units and ran out of money. The capital-heavy refinery consumed cash before the membrane was de-risked, and nothing reached commercial scale.
  • A UCLA milestone slipped and the license was downgraded to non-exclusive. The moat disappeared.
  • The IP never issued or the PCT national phase lapsed. With the know-how unlicensed, a fast follower replicated the chemistry.
  • Key-person risk. Hoek is the company on paper and is a full-time professor. His attention moved elsewhere and there was no second executive of equal standing.
  • The mineral economics never penciled. The 93% margins and the $459-a-tonne case proved fantasy, and the refinery that anchored the projections never reached positive unit economics.
